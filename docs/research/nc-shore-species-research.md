## 1. Purpose

This document consolidates the completed SaltBytes research for the initial North Carolina shore-fishing species set.

It combines:

- the species-selection rationale
- the approved pilot decision
- seven species-specific evidence profiles
- cross-species findings
- input-coverage analysis
- implementation-readiness analysis
- location-expansion priorities
- supported and prohibited interpretations
- unresolved evidence gaps
- the research decision register
- the source inventory

The document is intended to serve as the durable evidence base for later product, data, and implementation decisions.

It is not an opportunity-scoring specification. The separate
[species conditions scoring requirements](../requirements/species-condition-scoring.md)
define the approved product boundary without adding weights, thresholds, or
equations to this research package.

## 2. Research outcome

The approved initial set is:

1. Spanish mackerel
2. Red drum
3. Bluefish
4. Spotted seatrout
5. Kingfishes complex, with southern kingfish as the management indicator
6. Sheepshead
7. Florida pompano

The approved pilot is:

> **Spanish mackerel in shore-accessible North Carolina coastal habitat**

Two entries changed materially during detailed research:

- The original **southern kingfish** entry became **kingfishes complex, with southern kingfish as the management indicator** because recreational data are not sufficiently resolved to species.
- Florida pompano was retained but narrowed to **southern North Carolina sandy surf and ocean-pier habitat during the regional warm-water season**.

## 4. Research principles

### 4.1 Biological alignment is not fish presence

Environmental and habitat conditions may be broadly consistent with species use while the fish remain:

- absent
- offshore
- at another depth
- outside casting range
- at another structure
- following prey elsewhere
- temporarily passing through the region

### 4.2 Fishability is not biology

Wind, waves, rain, current, and access can determine whether an angler can fish effectively.

That does not prove those conditions increase biological activity.

### 4.3 Habitat suitability is not current habitat use

A suitable:

- pier
- grass bed
- sandy beach
- oyster reef
- inlet
- marsh edge
- bridge piling

does not prove fish or prey are currently present.

### 4.4 Physiological limits are not fishing optima

Laboratory and field evidence concerning:

- mortality
- cold stun
- lethal temperatures
- spawning temperatures
- growth conditions
- aquaculture performance

cannot be repurposed automatically into favorable fishing thresholds.

### 4.5 Spawning is not an opportunity bonus

Spawning evidence may describe:

- seasonal movement
- habitat use
- conservation sensitivity
- reproductive timing

It does not prove increased feeding or justify encouraging fishing on vulnerable aggregations.

### 4.6 Tide phase is not current

The same nominal tide phase can produce different:

- current speeds
- current directions
- water levels
- lags
- depth changes
- shoreline conditions

depending on site geometry and waterbody.

### 4.7 Missing data must remain missing

SaltBytes should not invent weak substitutes for unavailable observations.

Examples include:

- chlorophyll as baitfish
- rain as salinity
- tide phase as current
- sand as mole-crab presence
- structure type as fouling-community condition
- ocean SST as estuarine water temperature

## 5. Evidence methodology

### 5.1 Source hierarchy

The research prioritized:

1. North Carolina Division of Marine Fisheries data, profiles, assessments, management documents, reports, and proclamations
2. NOAA Fisheries and NOAA technical reports
3. Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission assessments and management documents
4. South Atlantic Fishery Management Council documents where relevant
5. Peer-reviewed research
6. University theses and technical reports where stronger evidence was unavailable
7. Current NCDMF recreational fishing reports as occurrence confirmation, not standardized abundance data

### 5.2 Evidence grades

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A | Strong direct evidence or authoritative North Carolina or regional evidence |
| B | Credible evidence with incomplete scope, indirect transferability, or unresolved confounding |
| C | Plausible mechanism or limited evidence unsuitable for deterministic use |
| X | Unsupported, contradicted, or too confounded for current use |

### 5.3 Transferability dimensions

Evidence was evaluated by:

- species
- life stage
- geography
- waterbody
- habitat
- season
- depth
- fishing mode
- study design
- environmental measurement
- whether the result concerned presence, feeding, survival, movement, harvest, or angler success

### 5.4 Fishery popularity versus management importance

These were evaluated separately.

A species may be management-important because of:

- stock concerns
- regulations
- cold-stun events
- local depletion
- spawning aggregation vulnerability

That does not prove it is one of the most frequently targeted shore species.

A species may be popular while having weak evidence for environmental predictability.

# Part I: Species Profiles

# 8. Spanish Mackerel Evidence Profile

## 8.1 Status

- **Species:** Spanish mackerel (*Scomberomorus maculatus*)
- **Research role:** Approved first scoring methodology pilot
- **Primary modes:** Surf, ocean pier, inlet-adjacent shore, open coastal water reachable from land
- **Primary assessment:** Broad seasonal and environmental alignment with shore-accessible occurrence
- **Not assessed:** Catch probability, bait-school detection, guaranteed presence, or harvest opportunity

## 8.2 Decision summary

Spanish mackerel is the strongest pilot because:

- it is genuinely accessible from North Carolina surf and piers
- it has substantial nearshore fishery relevance
- its broad coastal movement is associated with seasonal water temperature
- its initial inputs align well with existing SaltBytes data
- it has clean species identity
- its limitations can be explained clearly

The pilot must remain bounded because short-term presence depends heavily on mobile baitfish schools that SaltBytes does not observe.

## 8.3 North Carolina relevance

Spanish mackerel occur in coastal open water and are caught from:

- surf
- ocean piers
- nearshore boats
- inlet-adjacent waters

NCDMF reports recurring catches from beaches and piers.

State fishery tables repeatedly place Spanish mackerel among important nearshore-ocean species, although those tables combine shore and vessel modes.

That evidence establishes:

- regional importance
- shore accessibility
- seasonal recurrence

It does not establish shore-only abundance or hourly presence.

## 8.6 Seasonal movement and temperature

NOAA and NCDMF describe Atlantic Spanish mackerel as:

- overwintering farther south
- moving northward during spring
- occurring in North Carolina during the warm coastal season
- moving south again as waters cool

This supports broad thermal and seasonal alignment.

It does not support:

- one statewide arrival temperature
- one preferred SST
- a linear warmer-is-better relationship
- immediate arrival when a threshold is crossed
- identical timing north and south of Cape Hatteras

Public references to water near or above approximately 20°C are life-history context, not a validated shore-opportunity cutoff.

### Supported interpretation

> Current thermal conditions are broadly consistent or inconsistent with the regional season in which shore-accessible Spanish mackerel commonly occur.

### Prohibited interpretation

> Spanish mackerel have arrived because SST crossed a specific value.

## 8.7 Prey dependence

Spanish mackerel are strongly piscivorous.

Important prey include:

- anchovies
- menhaden
- sardines
- silversides
- mullet
- herring
- shrimp
- squid

NOAA diet research found fish dominated the stomach contents of Spanish mackerel sampled in the Carolinas.

Local prey distribution likely explains substantial short-term variability.

SaltBytes cannot currently observe:

- baitfish schools
- school distance from shore
- prey depth
- feeding activity
- whether fish are within casting range

The following are not valid substitutes:

- chlorophyll
- warm water
- tide phase
- cloud cover
- fishing reports
- generic bait season

## 8.8 Solar state and cloud cover

Spawning may occur at night during the warm season.

That does not establish a nighttime feeding preference.

No strong North Carolina adult shore evidence supports:

- dawn bonus
- dusk bonus
- sunny-day bonus
- overcast bonus
- night bonus

Solar state and cloud cover should remain available for retrospective analysis only.

## 8.9 Tide and current

Tides and currents may alter:

- prey transport
- current seams
- school position
- depth
- water clarity
- inlet exchange

No strong evidence establishes a universal preferred phase.

Prohibited claims include:

- incoming tide is better
- high tide improves Spanish mackerel opportunity
- faster current is better
- inlet proximity is automatically favorable

## 8.10 Wind, waves, and fishability

Wind and waves strongly affect:

- casting
- lure control
- line management
- surface-school visibility
- pier safety
- reach beyond the breakers

These are practical fishability effects.

The evidence does not support direct biological claims such as:

- onshore wind attracts fish
- calm water improves feeding
- rough water triggers activity
- post-storm conditions improve opportunity

## 8.12 Supported outputs

- The site and season are broadly consistent with shore-accessible Spanish mackerel.
- Current SST is typical, atypical, or uncertain for the regional seasonal context.
- Wind and waves make surf or pier fishing more or less practical.
- Confidence is limited because baitfish and local fish presence are unknown.
- These supported interpretations may contribute to an explainable species
  conditions score after the scoring methodology defines their roles.

## 8.13 Prohibited outputs

- Spanish mackerel will bite.
- Catch probability is high.
- A particular SST is optimal.
- Crossing a temperature proves arrival.
- Dawn or dusk is best.
- Incoming tide is better.
- Overcast conditions improve fishing.
- Favorable conditions prove fish are within casting range.

## 8.14 Research gaps

- North Carolina shore-mode estimates by beach and man-made shore
- nearshore SST representativeness
- regional timing north and south of Cape Hatteras
- adult diel behavior
- tide and measured current
- direct baitfish observations
- surf versus pier catchability
- sound-mouth applicability

## 8.15 Verdict

Spanish mackerel remains the approved first scoring methodology pilot.

Its defensible scoring question is:

> Are the location, season, regional thermal state, and practical fishing conditions broadly aligned with shore-accessible Spanish mackerel occurrence?

It must not answer:

> Will Spanish mackerel bite now?

# 9. Red Drum Evidence Profile

## 9.1 Status

- **Species:** Red drum (*Sciaenops ocellatus*)
- **Research role:** Approved initial species
- **Modes:** Surf, pier, inlet, sound, estuary, river, marsh edge, and shore-accessible structure
- **Primary problem:** Life-stage and habitat separation
- **Not assessed:** One universal statewide red drum opportunity

## 9.2 Decision summary

Red drum is the broadest species in the set but is not one clean ecological target.

The evidence describes:

- newly settled estuarine juveniles
- age-1 and age-2 coastal and estuarine fish
- slot-sized and subadult fish
- large Pamlico Sound adults
- offshore overwintering adults
- coastal spawning-stock fish

A future model must define the target context first.

## 9.3 North Carolina relevance

Red drum occur in:

- surf
- ocean piers
- inlets
- sounds
- river mouths
- coastal rivers
- marsh creeks
- shallow estuaries
- continental-shelf habitat

North Carolina supports a specialized catch-and-release fishery for large adults in Pamlico Sound.

Current NCDMF reports document red drum from:

- Brunswick County beaches
- southern piers and surf
- bays and creeks
- the Cape Fear River
- sounds and winter estuarine habitat

## 9.4 Life-stage contexts

```text
red_drum_context:
  - juvenile_estuarine
  - subadult_coastal_estuarine
  - adult_sound_inlet
  - adult_open_coast
```

The strongest first product scope is:

> **Slot-sized and subadult red drum in shore-accessible coastal and lower-estuarine habitat**

This avoids mixing:

- nursery juveniles
- offshore overwintering adults
- specialized trophy fisheries
- spawning-stock behavior

## 9.5 Seasonal movement

North Carolina tagging and telemetry research found:

- movement differed by age, region, and season
- younger coastal fish often moved alongshore
- fish in low-salinity tributaries moved with changing salinity
- adults moved from shelf overwintering areas into Pamlico Sound
- adults generally departed the sound in fall
- some adults migrated toward Virginia and returned

This supports life-stage-specific seasonal context.

It does not support one statewide migration calendar.

## 9.7 Water temperature

Temperature affects:

- growth
- metabolism
- seasonal movement
- habitat use
- prey
- physiological stress

The correct source depends on context:

- ocean SST for open surf
- sound temperature for Pamlico adults
- estuarine temperature for slot fish
- shallow-creek temperature for juveniles

A single SST field cannot represent all red drum habitats.

Prohibited interpretations include:

- one optimal red drum temperature
- warmer is always better
- laboratory lethal values define fishing conditions
- ocean SST represents creeks or sounds

## 9.8 Salinity and freshwater influence

North Carolina telemetry found tributary movement associated with fluctuating salinity.

Juvenile growth research suggested moderate salinity may reduce energetic costs in one estuary.

Red drum also tolerate broad salinity ranges.

These findings mean salinity can be:

- physiologically tolerated across a broad range
- behaviorally important
- correlated with discharge, temperature, depth, and prey
- different in importance by life stage

No universal favorable salinity is supported.

## 9.9 Tide, current, and diel behavior

Telemetry studies outside North Carolina found:

- site fidelity at low tide
- dispersal during some flood tides
- daylight-related flood movements
- substantial individual variation

These results do not establish an incoming-tide rule for North Carolina surf or estuaries.

Tide and solar state should remain analysis variables separated by:

- life stage
- site
- habitat
- season
- measured current

## 9.10 Habitat structure

Experimental research found structured habitat such as:

- seagrass
- oyster reef

reduced predator vulnerability for newly settled juveniles.

That evidence does not prove adult or slot-sized red drum are universally more catchable near structure.

Future metadata may include:

- marsh edge
- oyster
- seagrass
- dock
- piling
- jetty
- shoreline hardening
- bottom type

## 9.11 Prey

Red drum consume:

- crabs
- shrimp
- baitfish
- other benthic and water-column prey

Diet varies by:

- life stage
- location
- habitat
- season

SaltBytes does not observe prey.

The following cannot substitute:

- chlorophyll
- warm water
- tide phase
- recent rain
- spawning season
- generic bait season

## 9.12 Dissolved oxygen

Low oxygen can:

- reduce usable habitat
- alter movement
- cause mortality
- interact with temperature and depth

It may be valuable for:

- sounds
- creeks
- rivers
- stratified estuaries

It is unlikely to matter routinely in well-mixed open surf.

# 10. Bluefish Evidence Profile

## 10.1 Status

- **Species:** Bluefish (*Pomatomus saltatrix*)
- **Research role:** Approved initial species
- **Modes:** Surf, pier, bridge, inlet, sound, and coastal estuary
- **Primary problem:** Size-specific movement and mobile prey schools
- **Not assessed:** Blitz detection or exact school location

## 10.2 Decision summary

Bluefish is:

- highly migratory
- shore accessible
- strongly piscivorous
- divided into size classes with different distributions
- capable of using coastal and estuarine habitat
- observed across broad temperature ranges

A favorable season and SST do not establish that a school is within casting range.

## 10.3 Size-class contexts

```text
bluefish_context:
  - juvenile_estuarine
  - small_coastal
  - medium_coastal_estuarine
  - large_coastal
```

Recommended first scope:

> **Small and medium bluefish in shore-accessible coastal and inlet-connected North Carolina waters**

Large bluefish should remain separate because their distribution is generally farther north, east, and offshore.

## 10.4 Seasonal movement

Bluefish broadly:

- move northward during spring and summer
- move southward during fall and winter
- occur in North Carolina outside simple seasonal peaks
- vary by size class

NOAA analyses found lower relative southern catch rates during parts of midsummer and stronger southern contribution during cooler months.

This does not mean:

- July is always poor
- bluefish are absent in summer
- every size class moves together
- regional presence means shore accessibility

## 10.5 Temperature

Bluefish occur across broad and overlapping temperature ranges.

NOAA climate indicators involving 18°C concern spawning habitat and regional thermal conditions.

They do not define a fishing threshold.

North Carolina observations below 18°C demonstrate why the value cannot be used as a presence cutoff.

## 10.6 Climate-related distribution

Medium and large bluefish centers of distribution shifted northward and eastward during NOAA’s historical analysis.

The causes remain unresolved.

This supports:

- periodic review
- versioned climatology
- caution with fixed seasonal calendars

It does not prove bluefish have left North Carolina or that warming improves fishing.

## 10.7 Prey

Bluefish diets are dominated by fish.

Important prey include:

- bay anchovy
- menhaden
- spot
- butterfish
- herring
- silversides
- other schooling fishes

Prey location is probably more important to hourly shore access than many atmospheric variables.

SaltBytes does not observe:

- prey schools
- predator schools
- depth
- distance from shore
- school direction

## 10.8 Solar and diel evidence

A Hudson River juvenile study found:

- higher nearshore catches during daylight
- more offshore detections near sunset or night
- afternoon and evening feeding patterns

The study involved:

- young-of-year fish
- one estuary
- summer
- specific sampling gears

It cannot be converted into an adult North Carolina daytime rule.

## 10.9 Tide, current, and salinity

Tide and current may affect:

- prey transport
- water depth
- inlet exchange
- salinity
- fish position

No universal favorable phase is supported.

Bluefish use marine and brackish water across a broad salinity range.

No universal favorable salinity is supported.

# 11. Spotted Seatrout Evidence Profile

## 11.1 Status

- **Species:** Spotted seatrout (*Cynoscion nebulosus*)
- **Research role:** Approved initial species
- **Modes:** Sound, river, creek, lower estuary, bridge, dock, marsh edge, grass bed, inlet, and jetty
- **Primary problem:** Representative estuarine water data
- **Not assessed:** Ocean-SST-based estuarine opportunity

## 11.2 Decision summary

Spotted seatrout is the strongest estuarine species in the set.

It:

- can complete its full life cycle in estuaries
- supports major shore fisheries
- has strong North Carolina cold-stun evidence
- depends on local waterbody and habitat conditions
- requires water temperature measured in the correct waterbody and depth

Current SaltBytes marine SST is usually not representative.

## 11.3 Waterbody contexts

```text
spotted_seatrout_context:
  - shallow_sound
  - tidal_creek
  - coastal_river
```

## 11.4 Cold-stun evidence

North Carolina controlled research found:

- rapid exposure at or below approximately 4°C caused loss of equilibrium
- no fish survived two days at 3°C
- survival declined after prolonged exposure near 5°C
- exposure duration materially affected mortality
- salinity had little effect during severe prolonged cold
- historical cold metrics were associated with natural mortality

Additional North Carolina research found abundance declines associated with the number of days water remained below 7°C.

NCDMF management triggers include severe cold-duration criteria.

These are mortality and management signals.

They are not fishing-opportunity thresholds.

### Supported use

- severe cold exposure
- cumulative cold duration
- rapid cooling
- confirmed cold-stun status
- reduced confidence after mortality events

### Prohibited use

- 7°C as a fishing boundary
- water above a cold trigger as favorable
- immediate recovery after warming
- cold-stunned fish as opportunity

## 11.5 Ordinary temperature

Temperature is relevant to:

- habitat use
- metabolism
- spawning
- seasonal movement
- cold stress

Northern and southern populations show different physiological responses.

No universal North Carolina optimum is supported.

The first question must always be:

> Does the temperature source represent the habitat occupied by the fish?

Spotted seatrout use broad salinity ranges.

- water-mass identity
- prey
- spawning
- juvenile habitat
- local adaptation
- river-to-sound movement

No universal favorable direction is supported.

Rain is not a valid salinity measurement.

- deeper channels
- winter refuges

- diel changes

These findings are plausible but not enough for statewide North Carolina rules.

- cloud-cover bonus

is supported.

# 19. Shared confidence dimensions

Species conditions score outputs must expose confidence separately.

```text
species_identity_confidence
location_applicability_confidence
environmental_source_confidence
seasonal_evidence_confidence
habitat_data_confidence
biological_observation_confidence
fishability_data_confidence
overall_interpretation_confidence
```

One combined confidence score would hide why the result is uncertain.

# 20. Shared conceptual layers

## 20.1 Species-location applicability

Question:

> Is this species meaningfully associated with this location type?

## 20.2 Broad biological alignment

Question:

> Are season, habitat, and available environmental conditions broadly consistent with the species context?

## 20.3 Practical fishability

Question:

> Can an angler fish the location effectively and safely?

## 20.4 Unobserved local biology

Question:

> Which important facts remain unknown?

Examples:

- prey
- school presence
- fish depth
- structure occupancy
- local fishing pressure
- casting-range access

These layers may contribute to one explainable conditions score, but the
material contributions and unknowns must remain visible and confidence must
remain separate.

# 21. Universally prohibited interpretations

Unless materially stronger evidence is added, SaltBytes should not state:

- Catch probability is high.
- The fish will bite.
- Fish are present because conditions are favorable.
- One exact temperature is optimal.
- Warmer water is always better.
- Incoming tide is always better.
- High tide is always better.
- Dawn or dusk is universally best.
- Overcast conditions improve feeding.
- Barometric pressure predicts activity.
- Solunar periods predict opportunity.
- One wind direction attracts fish.
- Rough water improves biological opportunity.
- Calm water suppresses feeding.
- Recent rain creates a direct bite response.
- Spawning increases feeding.
- Structure guarantees fish.
- Sand guarantees prey.
- Grass guarantees spotted seatrout.
- A pier is biologically superior to surf.
- Chlorophyll proves baitfish.
- Regional stock status predicts local fish.
- Current regulations indicate abundance.
- Recent reports predict the next forecast period.
